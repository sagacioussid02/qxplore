Weights, parameters & layers — the knobs inside a neural network

Every AI model, from a toy like the one below to ChatGPT, is mostly just a huge pile of numbers and a fixed recipe for combining them. This page lets you turn those numbers yourself, then watch a computer turn them for you. That turning is training.

Layer

One row of the little calculator-circles (called neurons). Information flows left to right, one layer at a time.

Weight

A number written on a connecting line. It says how much that connection matters: a big positive weight amplifies the signal, a negative one flips it, zero ignores it.

Parameter

Any number the model is allowed to adjust — every weight, plus one extra per neuron called a bias (a built-in nudge that neuron adds on its own). When people say a model has “8 billion parameters,” they mean 8 billion of these numbers.

2 · Training = turning the knobs automatically

Nobody hand-picks good weights. Instead you show the model examples with known answers and measure loss — a single score for “how wrong are you, on average?” (0 = perfect). Then a simple rule nudges every knob a tiny step in whichever direction makes the loss smaller, and repeats. Try it: the weights below start out scrambled, so the model is confidently wrong.

3 · Now scale that up… a lot

Real models are the exact same idea — multiply, add, pass it on — just with vastly more knobs and many more layers stacked between input and output. Nothing new appears; only the count explodes.

3 layers. You have personally turned every one of its knobs.
Llama 3 8B (Meta, 2024) · 8,000,000,000 parameters
32 layers. About 890 million of these little weather brains’ worth of knobs.
GPT-3 (OpenAI, 2020) · 175,000,000,000 parameters
96 layers. About 19 billion weather brains’ worth of knobs.

The bars are drawn on a log scale (each equal step = 10× more), because on an honest straight scale the 9-parameter bar would be far thinner than a single pixel — the GPT-3 bar would need to stretch roughly 19 billion times wider.

A way to feel the numbers: if you read one parameter aloud per second, this demo’s 9 would take 9 seconds. Llama 3 8B’s would take about 254 years of non-stop reading. GPT-3’s would take about 5,500 years — longer than recorded history. Nobody sets these numbers by hand; that is exactly why training — the automatic knob-turning you just ran — has to exist.

The whole idea, in three lines

  • Layers are rows of simple calculators; each one re-mixes what the row before it produced.
  • Weights (plus biases) are the numbers doing the mixing — together they’re the model’s parameters, and they are all the model ever “knows.”
  • Training is just turning those numbers, a nudge at a time, until the loss on example answers gets small. Scale is the only difference between 9 parameters and 175 billion.
