1 / 4  Pick a two-qubit state

One qubit holds a mix of two outcomes, 0 and 1. Two qubits hold a mix of four: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩ and |11⟩ — the first digit is the first qubit, the second digit is the second. The state has four amplitudes, one per outcome, and their squares always add to 1. The worked example is two independent qubits: the first in |+⟩, the second in |0⟩.

3 / 4  Measure just one qubit

You do not have to read the whole register. Measure one qubit and only that qubit collapses — the other one survives, re-weighted to the outcomes that are still possible. Try it on the three-way mix (|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩) / √3: measuring the first qubit gives 0 two times in three, and then the second qubit is still 50/50; but if it gives 1, the second qubit becomes a certain 0.

4 / 4  A joint question: do the two qubits agree?

A measurement is a question you choose. Each qubit can be read in the Z basis (0 vs 1), the X basis (+ vs −) or the Y basis — the three Pauli bases. And a joint measurement can ask about a combination: Z⊗Z asks whether the two bits agree, without asking what either bit is. It has two answers — agree (00 or 11) or disagree (01 or 10) — and the probabilities come straight from the same four amplitudes.

Worked example: the first qubit is |+⟩ and the second is |0⟩, so the joint state is |+⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ = (|00⟩ + |10⟩) / √2. Measuring the register gives 00 with probability 50% and 10 with probability 50%; 01 and 11 never appear, because the second qubit is certain 0. The joint probabilities multiply because the qubits are independent. Measure only the first qubit and get 1: the second qubit is still |0⟩ — reading one independent qubit leaves the other untouched. Not every two-qubit state splits into a product like this one. The ones that do not are entangled — and that is the next lesson.
