A qubit holds a mix of 0 and 1. The mix slider sets how much of each outcome is in it — 36% is the worked example, (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩. The presets include two states, |+⟩ and |−⟩, that both measure 50/50 the plain way. Keep them in mind for step 3.

2 / 3  Measure it once — and watch it collapse

One measurement gives one classical bit: a 0 or a 1. It does not show you the mix — and it destroys it. The qubit becomes whichever outcome you saw. Measure the same qubit again and you get the same answer every time; only a fresh preparation can give a different one.

3 / 3  Measure many fresh copies — and change the basis

One measurement never reveals the probabilities. To see them, prepare the same state again and again and count the outcomes. And the measurement itself is a choice: the plain way reads in the Z basis (0 vs 1), while the X basis first applies an H gate and then reads — it asks whether the state is |+⟩ or |−⟩. |+⟩ and |−⟩ are twins in the Z basis (both 50/50), but in the X basis |+⟩ always gives 0 and |−⟩ always gives 1.

No measurements yet — pick a state and a basis, then measure 100 fresh copies.

Worked example: the state (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩ gives 0 with probability (3/5)² = 36% and 1 with probability (4/5)² = 64%. Suppose you measure and see 1: the qubit has collapsed to exactly |1⟩, and measuring it again gives 1 every time. The 36/64 split only shows up across many fresh preparations. And the twins: |+⟩ and |−⟩ both measure 50/50 in the Z basis, so a plain measurement cannot tell them apart — but in the X basis |+⟩ gives 0 every time and |−⟩ gives 1 every time. The right measurement can tell states apart that the plain one cannot.
