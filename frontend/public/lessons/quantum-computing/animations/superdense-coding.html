1 / 4  Pick the two bits to send

Alice and Bob already share an entangled pair in the Bell state Φ⁺ — Alice holds the first qubit, Bob the second. Alice wants to send two classical bits by shipping only her one qubit. She encodes the bits by touching only her own half: each of the four messages turns the pair into a different Bell state. The worked example is 10: a Z gate, turning the pair into Φ⁻.

2 / 4  Step through the protocol

The four Bell states are the whole alphabet: each one stands for a different two-bit message. Alice encodes by acting on her qubit alone, ships it to Bob, and Bob reads the message with a Bell measurement — a CNOT with Alice's qubit as the control, a Hadamard on Alice's qubit, then a plain measurement of both.

4 / 4  What if there was no shared pair?

A lone qubit can carry at most one classical bit — that is a hard limit. Without the shared pair, Alice can only put one bit on the qubit she ships, and the second bit has nowhere to go. Try it: Alice encodes only the first bit as |0⟩ or |1⟩ and Bob has to guess the second.

Press the button to see what arrives when Alice has no shared entangled pair.

Worked example: Alice wants to send 10. She applies Z to her half of Φ⁺, turning the pair into Φ⁻ = (|00⟩ − |11⟩) / √2, and ships her qubit to Bob. Bob's Bell measurement: the CNOT turns Φ⁻ into (|00⟩ − |10⟩) / √2, the Hadamard turns that into |10⟩, and he measures 10 — exactly the bits Alice sent. One qubit travelled; two bits arrived. The second bit rode on the entanglement that was set up in advance — and nothing travels faster than light, because Bob can read nothing until Alice's qubit physically reaches him.
